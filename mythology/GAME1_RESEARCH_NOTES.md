## Current research anchors

### Akan-speaking peoples

Akan research is useful for understanding how spiritual, social, ancestral, land, and human concepts can overlap without turning the world into a generic spell system.

Current evidence:

- A recent University of Johannesburg journal article examines Akan religion through the relationship between the divine, wild nature, and its domestication in social life.
- Cambridge Museum of Archaeology and Anthropology documents a Nyame Dua object and explains its ritual context as a place associated with offerings to Nyame.
- The Metropolitan Museum documents Asante-Akan akua 'ba figures and specific associations with fertility, beauty, wealth, and Asase Ya.
- University of Ghana research cautions against assuming one uncontested account of Nyame or creation narratives; historical sources and interpretations differ.

DOL implication:

- Treat Akan material as community-specific research, not as a universal “West African” system.
- Environmental/sacred-place references may inform social and environmental storytelling only after the exact adaptation context is recorded.
- Do not turn Nyame Dua, Asase Ya, ancestors, or other sacred concepts into generic loot, spells, or enemy types.

### Yoruba peoples

Yoruba research is useful as a comparative case for sacred landscapes, shrines, divination, kingship, and relationships between visible and spiritual life. It must not be merged with Akan material.

Current evidence:

- The Smithsonian's National Museum of African Art describes a Yoruba cosmological model connecting heaven/spirit and physical worlds, while also documenting ritual relationships with deities and spirits.
- The Metropolitan Museum documents Yoruba religious life around orisa and explains that specific shrines are sites of interaction between devotees and particular orisa.
- Princeton's museum collection documents Esu-related shrine and crossroads contexts and emphasizes the deity's specific religious role rather than a generic “trickster” label.

DOL implication:

- If Yoruba material is eventually adapted, preserve specific names, community context, and variation.
- A crossroads, shrine, divination object, or orisa cannot be treated as interchangeable fantasy scenery.
- “Esu = generic devil/trickster” is specifically not an acceptable DOL simplification.

## Research gates before final art

Before a sacred place, religious symbol, deity, spirit, ritual object, language phrase, or ceremonial practice enters final game art or mechanics:

1. Identify the specific community/tradition.
2. Record the source and exact evidence.
3. Check for variation or contested interpretation.
4. Classify it as DOCUMENTED, INTERPRETATION, DOL ORIGINAL, or UNVERIFIED.
5. Record the adaptation decision and representation risk.
6. Review the result before final art.
